Who Should Avoid This?

If you have a diagnosed medical condition such as diabetes, kidney disease, or an eating disorder, consult your healthcare provider before making significant dietary changes.

After weeks or months in a calorie deficit, your metabolism adapts. Reverse dieting is a structured approach to gradually increase calories post-cut, minimizing fat regain while allowing your metabolic rate to recover. Research shows that abrupt calorie increases lead to rapid fat storage, while a slow reverse minimizes this effect.     Frequently Asked Questions

    What is reverse dieting?

    Reverse dieting is a post-cut protocol where you systematically increase your calorie intake by 50-100 calories per week to transition from a deficit back to maintenance. The goal is to restore metabolic rate, normalize hunger hormones like leptin and ghrelin, and minimize fat regain.

    How fast should I increase calories after a cut?

    Most protocols recommend increasing by 50-100 calories per week, prioritizing carbohydrates first to restore glycogen and leptin levels. A typical reverse lasts 4-12 weeks depending on how long you were dieting and how aggressive your deficit was. Slower is generally safer for minimizing fat regain.

    Do I need to reverse diet?

    Not everyone needs a formal reverse diet. If your cut was short (4-6 weeks) and your deficit was modest (200-300 calories), you can likely return to maintenance within 1-2 weeks. However, if you dieted aggressively for 12+ weeks, a gradual reverse helps prevent excessive hunger, rapid weight regain, and metabolic suppression.
